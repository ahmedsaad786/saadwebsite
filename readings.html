Homework 2 First Reflection

I believe photography and image making is fundamental to this world both as an art form and a craft. So many inspiring pieces can be created, captured and altered, whether the reason is to elicit new meaning or build upon what was already understood. I primarily use photography as a means to cling to nostalgia, a feeling that humans often have a hard time ignoring. Whenever I’m out with my friends for a quick hangout or a long planned day, I often take photos throughout the day to commemorate those memories and look back on the time we spent together and the fun we had. Most of the time, the photos (and videos) are funny moments, which we send to each other to brighten up moods. One particular example is when we take a photo and make a sticker from it, which on its own pushes this idea of image making as an art form because the sticker we post is the original image altered. When we send these to make each other laugh, this illustrates the craft of image making, and how photography is important for the relationships we have.

Susan Sontag in her book On Photography describes how photography, or rather the act of photographing, replaces a sense of imposition. She states that “a camera is sold as a predatory weapon—one that’s as automated as possible, ready to spring” (Sontag 14). She goes on to elaborate how a camera equates to a gun, and uses the example of East African photography where those people hiding in bushes to photograph the wildlife are ever reminiscent of the hunters in days past. What’s important here is the idea of the camera being predatory, which is a topic of conversation in today’s age. The camera is absolutely a tool to peer, as the lens can be interpreted as a forbidden eye. The “wall” through which we see a photograph drops us in the perspective of the photographer, and there are circumstances in which photography can absolutely be violating for the photographed, or as Sontag puts it, “a soft murder” (15).

Although the camera is a weapon and tool of perversion, I strongly believe it can also be something good. Sontag goes on to talk about the role of nostalgia in camera craft, and I agree that photography actively promotes nostalgia. The main reason why we all take photos is to remember the moment, and to seek and share that moment with others. Photography generates a sense of community, one that I cling onto. In my experience, I’ve shared many photographs and have taken more pictures of others than of myself, because it brings me happiness to see those I hold close. I even share the group photos I take with my friends in our chat, so we can all look back and appreciate the moments we were together. All in all, while photography can absolutely be compared to a weapon or a gun, I think the benefits outweigh the negative connotations, though that is not to say the morality cannot be questioned. If taking pictures with my friends is something I enjoy doing, I intend to keep enjoying it.

Homework 4 Second Reflection

The recent popularity of AI and its use have blurred the lines for our perception of whether something is factually correct or generated by a computer. AI content is everywhere, from social media feeds like Instagram and Twitter, to written essays and papers, to web browsers and other programs pushing you to opt into their new ✨feature. I’ve witnessed that sparkle next to several buttons in an application and they’ve succeeded at ensuring I never click on it. I am heavily against the use of AI, particularly in terms of generative AI and the chaos it creates. Most professors these days have opened up and allowed the use of AI chatbots such as ChatGPT for feedback on writing assignments, yet I’ve always abstained. In my eyes, I don’t see the benefits of getting computer input as opposed to human feedback, let alone using a program to do my work for me.

Nesrine Malik in her article “With ‘AI slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster,” she states that “most of the time, AI slop is just content-farming chaos” (Malik 3). I wholeheartedly agree with Malik’s statement because I’ve witnessed the content-farming first hand while scrolling. Especially on sites like Facebook and Twitter, AI is extremely prevalent with little to no classification or disclaimer that parts of, if not the whole, video is computer generated in any way. Over time, AI has grown and evolved, and it’s slowly becoming harder to identify whether something was created by a human or if it was generated. There was a period of time in which images generated by AI had this orange hue, and was easily discernible. Sometimes people had more than 5 fingers on one hand, or didn’t have hands at all. Sometimes their hair was inconsistent, or the person seemed fine and yet the architecture in the background gave everything away.

I believe generated video is still easy to discern, because film has too many elements for AI to get consistently correct. With the gargantuan presence of short-form content, however, it’s possible to still fool those that wouldn’t know any better. Malik goes on to talk about entertainment algorithms being altered by the AI slop, and how “all media consumption, even for the most discerning users, becomes impossible to curate” (5). Malik says that viewers get immersed more into this subjective state, rather than an objective reality. There’s a disorientation, a blurring of the lines and of tones regarding content that is meant to be taken seriously. This all goes without mentioning how AI is used for propaganda purposes in addition to content farming– Scrolling on Twitter and Facebook especially, you can see how politically generative AI is used. For this one film project I was a part of in one of my classes, the director I was partnered with was comfortable using AI image generation to create a fake profile on Facebook that spewed the same propaganda that’s on the website normally. And what was crazy is the account never got taken down by Facebook, demonstrating a real life example of the “highly engaging content” Facebook pushes that Malik highlights in her article.

Homework 6 Third Reflection

Heela’s motherhood story struck out to me. She was somebody who survived multiple sexual assaults on repeated occasions, and was set to deliver a baby that she had little to no control regarding. Each of the motherhood stories start with a small text segment that provides context to the situation. It gives background on the topic at hand, whether it’s about rape, domestic abuse, or women’s healthcare. After this introduction, we begin the story, and for Heela it began with her feeling sick and becoming aware that she was actually pregnant. Heela wanted to have an abortion, and her friend who informed her that she may be pregnant was overcome with worry, as abortions could lead to Heela’s death. When this slide was presented, we are shown a medium close up shot of Heela’s friend who is worried for her, and it’s here that I believe the artistic style of these stories shine. The friend’s facial expressions are very distinct and convey her emotions to a degree unattainable by a regular photograph.

The use of artwork in this story helps with the immersion, putting readers into this world and helping them imagine themselves in Heela’s shoes. The soft style compliments the realism and almost portrays itself as a narrative that the reader can give their full focus to. And yet, we see photographs later in Heela’s story, which pull the reader back to reality. In my opinion, the use of photography in combination with the artistic style for her account only emphasizes the message of Heela’s story and raises its importance overall. This was a real event, with a real person who was significantly impacted by this event in her life. The reality check from using real photos is a beautiful demonstration of how vital it is to tell these stories, and share the crucial perspectives of these mothers. The use of text in a comic book style fashion also elevates the piece to put readers more into that story-like immersion, where the juxtaposition of photography and video hit harder. The construction of Heela’s story must be recognized because I don’t believe the same effects and messages could be conveyed if it were expressed exclusively through text.
